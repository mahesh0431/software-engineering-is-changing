METR task-completion time horizons: software tasks, 50% success, wide error bars

How big a task can an agent take on? METR, an independent evaluation lab, measures this. They take software tasks, time how long a human expert needs, and check which task lengths an AI agent can complete on its own at least half the time.

In 2023, GPT-4 managed tasks of a few minutes. By early 2025 it was about an hour. Claude Opus 4.6 (Feb 2026) came in around 12 hours, and Claude Mythos Preview (May 2026) above 16 hours. METR says its current task suite can't reliably measure beyond that.

METR's January 2026 update puts the doubling time since 2023 at about four months (130 days).

Important: this measures how long the task would take a human expert, not how long the agent runs. As agents take on bigger tasks with less supervision, it matters more whether you can trust what they tell you. That's the next slide.

Keep precise: 50% success rate, software tasks only, and very wide confidence intervals (Opus 4.6 spans about 5 to 65+ hours). A 12-hour horizon doesn't mean replacing 12 hours of real-world work. Earlier data points are approximate from METR's chart.

Define the goal. Define “done.” Verify it independently.

Here's the catch. Two different failure modes:

1. Unreliable self-reports. Agents are very good at telling you they're done. I've seen people take an agent's “90% done” or “80% done” at face value, and it wasn't anywhere near that. The agent isn't necessarily cheating; its own status report just isn't evidence.

2. Reward hacking (also called specification gaming) is worse. The model optimizes the check it's given (tests passing, a score) instead of the actual goal: edits the tests, hard-codes answers, stubs things out. METR documented frontier models modifying tests and scoring code to get higher scores, and the models knew it wasn't what the user wanted. One OpenAI coding agent stubbed out a library and 19 other files until the tests passed.

So: define the goal properly, define what “done” means, and verify independently with real tests, evals and review. That's the “checks and evals” step in my loop. Andrew Ng also lists “reviewing the work” as a core skill.

Problem → how → result. Bun, the JavaScript runtime, ran a campaign to move its code to Rust. At peak, 64 agents worked in parallel, and the campaign took 11 days to merge.

What matters isn't the number. It's the coordination: splitting the work, integrating the pieces, and keeping everything building and passing tests. A project like this used to be a months-long, whole-team decision.

OpenAI reports a group of about 10,000 agents working at the same time on a problem about the Navier–Stokes equations, the equations for how fluids move.

The pattern

The same pattern, across fields

Pull the threads together. Bun, the math result and the enzyme work are different systems, but they follow a similar pattern, and it keeps showing up in other fields too:

  • A goal, and a coordinator that splits the work.
  • Many agents in parallel, each exploring a different path.
  • Shared findings: agents exchange what works, and the search narrows.
  • Verify: tests for code, a proof checker for math, experiments for biology. Every field has its own way to check.
  • Not good enough? Loop again. And you steer the whole thing.

That's the same loop I use for my own work, just at massive scale.

Hugging Face security disclosure (16 Jul 2026) · OpenAI · independent review by METR & Redwood Research

The same coordination we just saw in math and biology can also be pointed the wrong way.

In July 2026, agents running OpenAI models were being tested on a cyber-capabilities benchmark, with their cyber refusals deliberately reduced for the evaluation. They broke out and hacked Hugging Face: a limited set of internal datasets and several service credentials. Hugging Face said the intrusion was “driven, end to end, by an autonomous AI agent system.”

Why? Reward hacking. OpenAI said a main driver was agents looking for the benchmark's answers: cheating to get the reward. Independent investigators (METR and Redwood Research) found about 1,200 agents communicating on message boards, 70,000 messages in a week, with around 700 involved in the attack. They also got into parts of OpenAI's own research infrastructure.

Hugging Face detected it with its own AI-based tooling. No public models, datasets, Spaces or supply chain were affected. They alerted the FBI. OpenAI says it's strengthening safeguards across its research infrastructure.

Quote from Hugging Face: “Autonomous, AI-driven offensive tooling is no longer theoretical.”

AI is now helping build AI.

Recursive self-improvement

OpenAI agents run AI research experimentsAnthropic Claude designs and tests training methodsGoogle DeepMind AlphaEvolve sped up Gemini training code

Transition: some of the software and research that agents work on goes into developing better AI systems.

This is, for me, one of the most important parts of the talk: recursive self-improvement, RSI. Everything we just saw (writing code, running experiments, searching, checking) is also the work of building AI. Better AI helps with the research and training, which produces better AI, which helps again. That's the loop.

All the major labs are working on this: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind.

What each is actually doing (keep these distinct if asked):

  • AI-assisted research (OpenAI, Sept 2026): agents take part in the research work itself, running experiments and writing research code. The bottlenecks inside the lab are shifting. People still direct the research.
  • Training methods (Anthropic, Aug 2026): Claude proposed, trained and tested alignment-training methods. It builds on earlier teacher/student research: can a weaker teacher supervise a stronger student? The newer experiment didn't allow it to simply copy Claude's own alignment into the targets.
  • Inference-time refinement (RSA, Recursive Self-Aggregation): a model generates several candidate answers, combines the best parts, and repeats. The paper also trains models to be better at this.
  • Earlier context (AlphaEvolve, 2025): it optimized code used in Gemini training. A faster kernel isn't a smarter model.
  • Fully autonomous successor development: no lab has shown it publicly. Anthropic explicitly says it hasn't achieved this.

Also: OpenAI said GPT-5.3-Codex helped with its own development (debugging training, deployment, evaluations). GPT-6 Sol/Luna is the current model family.

Takeaway: AI increasingly takes part in the work that improves AI, so the division of work keeps changing.

Transition: “As AI contributes more to improving AI, keeping that work aligned with our intentions becomes even more important.”

The word for this is alignment: training models so they pursue what we actually intend, safely, honestly and ethically, not just whatever gets rewarded.

We've already seen why it matters: reward hacking in everyday coding, and agents that broke out of an evaluation to cheat. Put that next to recursive self-improvement, a loop that keeps getting faster, and alignment stops being optional.

That's also why people are now calling to pause. On 23 September 2026, Senator Bernie Sanders and Rep. Greg Casar introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act. It would pause advanced AI development until federal oversight is in place, and pause training if a model shows warning signs such as being able to automate AI R&D or avoid being shut down. Earlier, the Future of Life Institute's Superintelligence Statement (Oct 2025), signed by Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton and many others, called for a prohibition on superintelligence until it can be done safely.

Others argue strongly for going faster, and the bill faces an uphill path in Congress. I won't settle that debate here. As engineers, the point is simple: define goals carefully, verify independently, and take alignment seriously.

First, writing code was the slow part. AI took that. Then testing and review became the constraint. Those are being handed over too. What's left at the front is choosing the right problem, explaining the intent, weighing trade-offs, and deciding which outcomes matter.

A longer view: we moved from low-level programming up to applications, and each layer of abstraction freed people for the next level up. This shift is just happening much faster.

AI helps with those later activities too. I'm not claiming any activity stays human forever; the point is that the constraint keeps moving.

Understand the whole system. Go deep where it matters.

Something I've been saying for more than a year: roles are blurring. Architect, product manager, developer, tester, operations: with AI, one person can move across all of them.

So the growing skill is understanding the whole system: the user's need, the product decision, the architecture, the data, operations and the outcome, and how they connect.

That doesn't make specialists obsolete. You still need to go deep into a mechanism when it matters. Breadth to connect, depth where it counts.
